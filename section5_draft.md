## 5. Future Enhancements, Sustainability, Governance, and Ongoing Maintenance

### 5.1 Future enhancements and innovation opportunities

The PoC in Section 4 uses a transparent linear/seasonal regression specifically because it is easy to justify at proof-of-concept stage; the clearest near-term enhancement is to replace it with a proper time-series/ML forecasting pipeline (for example, gradient-boosted trees or a dedicated forecasting library, retrained on a schedule) once the curated aggregate data has accumulated enough history to support it. A further step is streaming/near-real-time anomaly detection on the voltage and frequency signals themselves (rather than only batch fault-rate summaries), which would let MES act on emerging substation issues within seconds rather than at the next batch cycle. Looking further ahead, MES is well placed to build a **digital twin** of its grid — a live, continuously-updated model combining consumption, generation, and network-state data — to simulate the effect of new EV/solar adoption or demand-response programmes before rolling them out. Edge AI (running lightweight anomaly-detection models directly on substation gateways, rather than only pre-filtering data as in the current design) is a natural extension of the edge layer already proposed in Section 3.

### 5.2 Sustainability

A big data platform of this scale has a real energy footprint, which sits awkwardly next to MES's identity as an energy-services provider — this tension is worth naming explicitly rather than glossing over. Practical levers include: right-sizing and auto-scaling compute so batch jobs (such as the PySpark aggregation demonstrated in the PoC) are not left running against idle, over-provisioned clusters; scheduling non-urgent batch workloads for times/regions where the underlying electricity grid is carrying a cleaner generation mix ("carbon-aware scheduling"); and applying disciplined data-lifecycle policies (moving cold historical telemetry to lower-energy archive storage tiers, and deleting data once its regulatory retention period has lapsed) so storage growth does not run unchecked. None of this replaces the primary sustainability value MES delivers through its core business (enabling solar integration, EV charging, and demand-side flexibility); it is about ensuring the data platform that supports that mission does not undermine it.

### 5.3 Governance

Sustained data quality and trust require governance structures, not just the technical controls described in Section 3. This includes: a data catalogue and named data-stewardship roles for each major domain (metering, EV, solar, billing) so that ownership of data quality is unambiguous; formal onboarding standards/schema contracts for new device vendors, so the unit- and format-inconsistency problems reproduced in the PoC's synthetic dataset are caught before a new vendor's data reaches production, not after; periodic data-protection impact assessments for any new use of customer-level data; and a lightweight governance board or forum (spanning IT, compliance/legal, and grid-operations stakeholders) that reviews new data uses against MES's regulatory obligations before they go live.

### 5.4 Ongoing maintenance

Recommended operational practices include: pipeline observability with alerting on ingestion lag, schema-violation rates, and job failures (directly resolving the brief's "limited visibility into pipeline performance"); scheduled model-performance monitoring with drift detection, so a forecasting model's accuracy degrading over time (as the customer base, weather patterns, or EV/solar adoption shift) is caught proactively rather than discovered via a bad forecast; regular security review (access-control audits, penetration testing, dependency/vulnerability scanning) given MES's role in critical infrastructure; and periodic disaster-recovery testing of the multi-region resilience design, since an untested failover plan is not a reliable one.
