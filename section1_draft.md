## 1. Data Requirements, Big Data Challenges, and Ethical/Regulatory Considerations

### 1.1 Data acquisition

MetroEnergy Solutions (MES) acquires data from six operationally distinct sources: smart electricity meters, EV charging stations, solar panel monitoring systems, IoT sensors in substations, customer billing platforms, and maintenance management systems. These sources differ sharply in velocity and format. Smart meters and substation sensors generate continuous, high-frequency telemetry (voltage, current, frequency, fault alerts) that must be acquired in near real time to support grid stability monitoring; billing and maintenance systems, by contrast, operate in scheduled daily batches. A single acquisition strategy therefore cannot serve both needs. A practical approach is a dual-path ingestion model: a streaming path (for example, a publish/subscribe message layer) for continuous telemetry, and a batch/file-based path for daily extracts from billing and maintenance platforms. Acquisition is complicated further by MES's "fragmented regional data platforms" and "inconsistent data formats across vendors." Third-party smart meters, EV charge-point operators, and solar inverter manufacturers each expose their own protocols and schemas (for example, some use kWh, others Wh; timestamp formats and time-zone handling differ by vendor). This is precisely the *variety* dimension of big data, and it means acquisition cannot be treated as a purely technical exercise: it requires a data contract or schema registry that vendors and legacy grid systems are onboarded against, with validation applied at the point of ingestion rather than downstream. The PoC in Section 4 demonstrates this concretely — the synthetic dataset deliberately contains a legacy-vendor unit mismatch (Wh reported as if it were kWh) and inconsistent timestamp strings, and the PySpark ingestion script enforces an explicit schema and normalises both issues before the data is trusted for analysis.

### 1.2 Storage

MES's data spans several storage profiles that should not share one design. High-volume, append-only telemetry (meter readings, substation sensor logs) is best suited to object storage or a distributed file system organised into raw/curated/analytics zones (a "medallion" or multi-zone pattern), because it is written once, read many times, and does not need transactional guarantees. Customer and billing records, by contrast, are relational, are updated, and carry personal and financial data, so they belong in a managed relational or semi-structured database with strong consistency and access controls. Time-series data (voltage, frequency, generation output) benefits from storage or query engines optimised for time-series access patterns.

The *volume* of smart-meter and IoT data across "the UK and Europe" also has a direct storage-tiering implication: recent data that feeds near-real-time dashboards needs to sit on higher-performance storage, while older data retained only for regulatory or historical-trend purposes can move to lower-cost cold/archive tiers. This tiering decision is not just a cost optimisation; it is also a compliance lever, since data-retention limits under data-protection law (see 1.4) are easier to enforce when data lifecycle rules are attached to storage zones rather than applied ad hoc.

### 1.3 Processing and analysis

Two processing regimes are required concurrently: stream processing for near-real-time use cases (fault detection, voltage-anomaly alerting, live grid-load monitoring) and batch processing for use cases that tolerate latency (daily billing runs, monthly consumption reporting, seasonal trend analysis, and demand forecasting). The PoC in Section 4 illustrates the batch/analytical side of this using PySpark: it aggregates raw hourly telemetry into hourly load profiles, seasonal trends, and a peak-demand forecast — outputs that are valuable but do not need to exist within milliseconds of the underlying reading being recorded. A production MES pipeline would pair this with a streaming layer (for example, structured streaming or a managed stream-processing service) so that fault alerts and voltage excursions are actioned within seconds rather than waiting for the next batch cycle.

Analytically, the organisation's stated goals — forecasting analytics, monitoring generation/consumption/grid stability, and system-wide pipeline visibility — require MES to move beyond descriptive reporting toward predictive and operational analytics: peak-demand prediction, anomaly detection on voltage/frequency signals, and pipeline observability metrics (ingestion lag, failed-record rates, schema-violation counts) that directly answer the brief's concern about "limited visibility into pipeline performance."

### 1.4 Big data challenges (the five Vs, applied to MES)

- **Volume** — hundreds of thousands to millions of readings per day once smart meters, EV chargers, and substation sensors across multiple countries are all reporting; this was reflected directly in the PoC's dataset design (Section 4), which scales to roughly 876,000 raw readings from a modest 100-meter sample.
- **Velocity** — telemetry arrives continuously, while billing/reporting arrives in daily batches; the two cannot be forced into a single latency budget.
- **Variety** — structured billing records, semi-structured device telemetry, unstructured maintenance notes, and external weather feeds must all be reconciled, plus the vendor-format inconsistencies discussed in 1.1.
- **Veracity** — sensor dropout, duplicate delivery, unit mismatches, and faulty-firmware readings (all reproduced synthetically in the PoC) mean raw data cannot be trusted without a cleansing stage; veracity failures that reach a forecasting model silently degrade its accuracy.
- **Value** — the commercial and operational point of collecting all this data is to enable forecasting, demand-response, predictive maintenance, and regulatory reporting; a big data platform that cannot demonstrably support these outcomes has not delivered value, regardless of how much data it stores.

### 1.5 Technical considerations

Beyond ingestion/storage/processing, MES needs: schema governance and a data catalogue so that fragmented regional platforms converge on shared definitions; interoperability standards (open APIs, common protocols such as MQTT for IoT telemetry) to integrate legacy grid-management systems and third-party device vendors without bespoke point-to-point integrations for every vendor; and observability tooling (pipeline monitoring, data-quality dashboards, alerting on ingestion failure) to resolve the "limited visibility" constraint named in the brief.

### 1.6 Ethical considerations

Smart-meter data is a proxy for household behaviour — occupancy patterns, daily routines, even lifestyle inferences can be derived from consumption curves at sufficiently fine granularity. MES therefore has an ethical obligation to apply data-minimisation (collecting and retaining only what each use case genuinely needs), to consider privacy-preserving aggregation for outputs that do not need household-level granularity (e.g., regional load forecasting does not require identifiable household curves), and to be transparent with customers about what is collected and why. There is also a fairness dimension: forecasting and demand-response programmes should be evaluated for whether they disadvantage particular customer segments (for example, customers without EVs or solar being implicitly deprioritised in a system optimised around flexible/prosumer load).

### 1.7 Regulatory considerations

As a provider operating across the UK and Europe, MES sits under overlapping regulatory regimes. Data protection is governed by the UK GDPR/Data Protection Act 2018 domestically and the EU General Data Protection Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2016/679) for its European operations, both of which impose requirements on lawful basis for processing, data-subject rights, cross-border transfer safeguards, and breach notification — all directly relevant given MES processes granular, potentially personally-identifiable consumption data. As an energy provider, MES is additionally subject to sector-specific regulatory oversight (in the UK, Ofgem; at a European grid level, coordination frameworks such as those overseen by ENTSO-E) covering metering accuracy, supply reliability, and grid-code compliance. Security expectations are shaped by standards such as ISO/IEC 27001 for information security management, and, given MES's role in critical national infrastructure, sector-specific critical-infrastructure/cybersecurity obligations (for example, NIS-type regulation in the UK/EU) are likely to apply to substation and grid-control systems specifically.
